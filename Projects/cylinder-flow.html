Experimental fluid mechanics / Wind tunnel

Cylinder in a uniform flow

Pressure distribution, wake velocity, and drag estimation | ME 347 | Cal Poly San Luis Obispo

Wind Tunnel Testing Pressure Measurement Wake Survey Numerical Integration Experimental Validation

Worked with a team of four on this lab, and I measured how air moves around a brass cylinder and compared two independent drag estimates. The pressure result matched published behavior; the wake result did not, making measurement quality as important as the final number.

01

Test setup and method

One cylinder, two ways to measure drag

A 1.50 in brass cylinder was mounted perpendicular to the flow in the Cal Poly mechanical engineering wind tunnel. After locating the stagnation point, the team recorded surface pressure in 10-degree increments from 0 to 180 degrees.

A Pitot-static probe on an x-y traverse then sampled the wake at 17.5 in and 25.0 in downstream. Ambient conditions and free-stream dynamic pressure established air density, velocity, and a cylinder Reynolds number of 3.49 × 104.

02

Surface pressure

The expected signature of separation

The stagnation point produced the largest pressure coefficient, Cp = 1.03. Pressure fell as the flow accelerated around the cylinder, reached Cp = -1.54 near 70 degrees, and remained negative across the rear surface—a broad low-pressure region consistent with separated flow.

Interpretation: the nearly flat, negative pressure on the back side indicates that the boundary layer separated and left a wide turbulent wake rather than recovering to the ideal-flow pressure distribution.
03

Wake development

Velocity deficit and downstream recovery

Both wake surveys showed their lowest normalized velocity near the cylinder centerline and recovery toward free-stream velocity away from the wake. The profile farther downstream appeared broader and smoother, which is consistent with turbulent mixing.

04

Drag comparison

A good result and a useful discrepancy

Numerically integrating the surface pressure distribution gave CD = 1.256, close to the expected value of approximately 1.2. Integrating the wake momentum deficit produced much lower values, so those results were treated as evidence of an incomplete or inconsistent wake survey—not as validated drag coefficients.

05

Engineering takeaway

Validate the method, not only the arithmetic

The experiment connected surface pressure, separation, wake formation, and pressure drag in one test. More importantly, comparing independent methods exposed a measurement problem that a single calculation would have hidden.

A stronger repeat test would re-zero the transducer before each traverse, verify probe alignment, extend the survey beyond the full velocity deficit, and repeat multiple passes to quantify the unsteadiness of the wake.
