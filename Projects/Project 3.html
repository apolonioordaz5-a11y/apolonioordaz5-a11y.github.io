Product design · Prototype · 2025

Built a cart that moves and flips a 5-gallon water jug for you.

Lifting and tipping a heavy water bottle into place is awkward and risky. I designed and built a wheeled carrier that transports the bottle and inverts it using leverage instead of a straight lift.

The problem

Lifting and inverting a full 5-gallon bottle strains the back and can slip out of control.

What I did

Interviewed users, sketched concepts, and built a wheeled frame with a crate mount and a long lever handle.

The result

A working prototype that moves the bottle and tips it into position without an unsupported lift.

Full engineering write-up

The project addressed a physical handling problem: moving and inverting a heavy 5-gallon water bottle without relying on a direct lift. I worked through the complete design cycle—from user interviews and concept sketches to fabrication, testing, and refinement.

01

Problem framing

Design around the user, not only the load

User interviews focused the project on the difficult parts of the task: lifting the bottle, keeping it controlled during transport, and rotating it into position without creating a new stability or pinch-point problem.

Those observations turned a broad idea into three design priorities: reduce the need for unsupported lifting, keep the bottle positively located during movement, and give the user enough leverage to control inversion.

TransportMove the bottle on a stable wheeled base
ControlKeep the load retained through the motion
LeverageUse frame geometry to reduce handling effort
02

Concept development

Translate handling needs into a buildable frame

The selected concept combined a wheeled base, an angled handle for leverage, and a crate mount sized to hold the bottle during transport and inversion. The sketch established the main load path and component relationships before material was cut.

03

Prototype and evaluation

Test the complete interaction, not a static object

The functional prototype was evaluated during movement and inversion rather than judged only by whether it could hold the bottle. Hands-on testing considered transport stability, control of the load, ergonomics, usability, and safety.

User feedback informed refinements and confirmed that the frame geometry could support the intended handling sequence. The build also exposed the practical importance of wheel placement, retention, and clearance around the bottle and operator.

Engineering takeaway: A successful handling aid must manage the user’s motion and the load’s motion at the same time. Stable geometry, retention, and leverage are one connected system.
