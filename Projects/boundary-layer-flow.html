External flow / Boundary-layer measurement

Boundary layer growth over a flat plate

Pitot-static velocity profiles, integral thicknesses, and drag estimation | ME 347 | Cal Poly San Luis Obispo

Wind TunnelPitot-Static TraverseBoundary LayersNumerical IntegrationDrag Estimation

Worked with a team of four on this lab, and I measured the velocity field developing over a flat plate. The profiles let us track boundary-layer growth, calculate displacement and momentum thickness, and compare the resulting drag estimate with laminar and turbulent theory.

01

Traverse the velocity profile

Two stations, one developing boundary layer

A Pitot-static tube moved outward from the plate in small increments at x = 11.5 cm and x = 21.5 cm. Manometer deflection was converted to dynamic pressure and local velocity, with the plateau defining the free stream.

The free-stream speed was approximately 20.03 m/s. Boundary-layer thickness was identified where the local velocity reached 99% of that value.

02

Growth downstream

Viscous effects spread farther from the wall

The measured boundary layer grew from 2.775 mm to 4.48 mm between the two stations. Displacement thickness increased from 0.435 mm to 0.582 mm, while momentum thickness increased from 0.194 mm to 0.330 mm.

Those changes are consistent with a developing boundary layer: progressively more fluid is slowed by the wall, increasing both the effective displacement of the outer flow and its momentum deficit.

03

Profile against theory

Measurements fell between ideal laminar and turbulent behavior

At both stations, the experimental profile rose more rapidly than the Blasius laminar solution and followed the turbulent power-law curve more closely through much of the layer. It did not match either ideal model exactly.

04

Drag from momentum thickness

A velocity profile becomes a force estimate

Numerical integration of each profile produced momentum thickness, which was then used to estimate plate drag. The calculated drag force increased downstream from 0.00463 N to 0.00788 N as the momentum deficit accumulated.

The drag coefficient remained close to the laminar prediction even though the profile shape appeared more turbulent, reinforcing that the layer was in transition rather than fully developed in either regime.

05

Takeaway

Model disagreement identified the flow regime

The experimental thickness stayed between the laminar and turbulent predictions, while the velocity shape leaned turbulent and the drag metrics leaned laminar. Together, those results point to a transitional boundary layer. The largest uncertainty came from resolving velocity very near the wall, compounded by wind-tunnel interruptions during the traverse.

Engineering takeaway: no single metric defined the regime. Comparing profile shape, integral thicknesses, Reynolds number, and drag produced a more defensible conclusion than any one result alone.
